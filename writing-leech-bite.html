Harassment is like a leech bite.

Working in a male-dominated field, I found it extremely hard to talk about women’s dilemma. I still remember the awkward faces when I brought up gender topics in our group meeting. Even here at Silicon Valley—“the center of universe”, I experienced many irritating moments when some men commented on my appearance/body shape in the workplace, talked to me with deprecative words, or intentionally excluded me in conversations. “Microaggressions are like mosquito bites”—it’s annoying but not deadly. In comparison, harassment is like a leech bite. It will leave a hole on your skin that keeps bleeding.

I have always been self-aware as a strong and assertive woman. I had naive confidence that sexual harassers would be intimidated by my personality. Obviously, life just slapped heavily in my face. There is some misconception of harassment—it’s a pitfall on the road or an exploding capacitor on the circuit board that you could immediately detect. Actually, it’s more like a dead bug ground together with your coffee beans. You didn’t even notice the extra “flavor”, but the virus would cause year-long nausea and diarrhea. Harassment may come disguised as kindness, in my case, friendship. With the assumption that he was kind, at first I tried to convince myself his misbehavior was okay, “Was my communication unclear? Were there any hidden culture barriers? Could it just be because of his low emotional intelligence?” As an engineer, I kept adjusting my solutions according to the situation. I initiated an open conversation with him. I moved my desk to a new office. I closed my social media accounts. I talked to the Title IX office. I moved to a new apartment. The weirdest part was that I sought all the available help but the situation kept swelling and festering. Every time I thought it’s over and I could finally regain my inner peace, the harassment came back even more twisted. After several repetitions, I lost the ability to expect anything positive. Like the cliche, a neverending nightmare. (Inappropriately, an academic joke/sarcasm just popped up in my mind: For experiments, it’s “better” as long as your measurements don’t get worse.)

Then, focusing on work became impossible. I couldn’t stop questioning, when did it go wrong? How bad can it become? My brain was fully occupied without any extra bandwidth for research. (In retrospect, when things started to go really wrong, I felt confused, not angry, nor sad. Later I learned that confusion is a common reaction to abuse/violence.) For months, I couldn’t fall asleep, trying to figure out the root cause, hoping that I could find a perfect solution. In the end, unfortunately, sexism was the only thing I found—pure malice against women for no reason. Just because I’m female, I shouldn’t have rejected his manipulation and superiority. As a cost, he’d sabotage my career in front of the authority. I found myself interesting—I didn’t feel sad for the distress I experienced. But upon the moment when I realized the “friendship” never existed—it’s just the disguise of sexism, I felt suffocated. The whole thing was completely beyond my understanding and soon spinned out of control. Since it’s an outlier that never appeared in my training data, I knew I couldn't predict it. The only way to minimize my loss was to assume the worst. Whenever I got out of the office or walked alone, I compulsively looked around to ensure nobody was around. I became super vigilant like a prey in darkness. Alcohol was the only thing to help me relax.

Unexpectedly, my relationships with others were also infected. At the beginning, I kept the conflicts only to myself so that the toxic vibe didn’t spread to larger groups. It’s also hard because the manipulative side of humanity I saw was invisible to others. How to tell them that “kind” guy hurt me intentionally? I was soon overwhelmed by the mental stress. Even now, I could hardly find the “sweet spot” to describe my pain. On one hand, if I calmly analyzed my situation like an outsider, my stress seemed trivial and exaggerated—her pain was still manageable, she could take more. On the other hand, if I couldn’t contain my emotions, others might see me as a crazy burnt-out woman. Anyway, the mental stress pushed me to talk to others, which turned out to be also hurtful. My friends said I was traumatized and thus overreacting. My therapist said my brain might have made a mistake—he might just be passing by, not stalking (even at 7:55am on a hidden side path?). The dangerous cues I spotted were rejected by people I trusted, and meanwhile I had to prove my sanity. Though not true, I felt abandoned to face an approaching bear, when I asked them for help and support. For some moments, I was quietly waiting for a disaster so that somebody would believe me.

Weirdly, under extreme fear, I pretended I was happy and feeling safe. I was anxiously calculating backup plans to stay safe, meanwhile in front of people, I was a jumpy rabbit running to seminars, joking about my heavy drinking issue, and celebrating my graduation. Now I realize it might be a self-protection mechanism to stay functional—I had to submit my manuscript, my thesis, and all the paperwork for graduation. It turns out you can deceive your own brain, but only temporarily.

The leech bite left a hole that keeps bleeding. I never thought the harassment lasted for that long, and I underestimated the time I need for recovery. My positivity and motivation were burnt away in that huge fire. People told me, “You have a bright future.” Logically, yes; it’s an era of meritocracy and I have a perfect degree. But things no longer add up. It’s also possible that some men would sabotage me and threaten me, like it happened, right? I became numb to my own feelings as well as others—I have difficulty reacting to others’ emotions and responding to simple messages. I still feel insecure if some innocent people accidentally leak my info (e.g. physical location), which had happened before. Alcohol has destroyed my attention span for deep work and the reconstruction is hard. Being positive, I did learn something—I’m better at identifying women-hostile working environments.

“A trouble is a private matter. An issue is a public matter.” (C. Wright Mills, The Sociological Imagination) Where to place my case in the normal distribution? How far does it deviate from the mean? Sadly, when I look around, I see the collective pain shared by my female peers. My trouble wasn’t even that severe compared to theirs. Some were muted by fear, or even hunted after by the whole department. I can’t imagine how destructive that can be. More often, we experience [misogynistic] microaggressions, like ignoring our opinions or taking our credits. The hindrance isn’t just the second shift, it’s the hidden bias of sexists.

I struggled a lot to share my story, even right now. So I asked my friend for support. She said better no, few will truly understand. Yes, indeed. We humans have such poor imaginations towards others’ pain. Just to clarify, I’m not seeking any sympathy. I’ve heard “sorry” for too many times over the past two years: Now it’s completely meaningless to me. “Emotion without action is pointless.” (The Power of Women, Denis Mukwege) I encourage people to intentionally increase their sensitivity to discriminative cues, and learn what respectful behaviors are like.

There is no quick solution to the entrenched stereotypes and discrimination. She is brave and powerful. Just be mindful.
